If directors expect their internal communications to be disclosed, they might self-censor their dissent against management.

If management expects books and records to be disclosed, they might curate the content to remove or soften evidence of dissent or wrongdoing.

Suppose one outside director, Elena, is skeptical of a management proposal. She has a fiduciary duty to act on her belief, but opposing management could hurt her career.

Directors who monitor management aggressively fare worse in the market for board seats. Those who ingratiate themselves with the CEO fare better.

Westphal & Stern (2007); Bebchuk & Fried, Pay Without Performance 36 (2004).

It is cheaper for her to build a coalition behind the scenes.

One conversation at a time.

But opposition-building is cheaper only so long as management cannot watch it happen.

Disclosure makes opposition-building transparent and therefore more difficult. This is a cost for corporate governance.

In the paper, I study two underexamined effects of the transparency of corporate records.

Economists and political scientists have shown that anticipated disclosure can chill candid deliberation, induce conformity, and push real discussion to informal channels. Corporate law should reckon with these findings.

But only if content is truly hidden from management

Section 220 of the Delaware General Corporation Law gives shareholders the right to inspect corporate books and records if they have a proper purpose for doing so.

The new version of the statute, enacted in 2025, creates a two-tier system.

For core board-level records, shareholders must show that they have a credible basis for investigating wrongdoing. A minimal burden.

For all other records, shareholders must pass two additional filters. The evidentiary burden is higher — clear and convincing evidence.

Is this reading doctrinally sound?

The phrase is borrowed. The first attempts to read the new statute reached for attorney work product, which uses “compelling need” for a similar two-tier structure and rests on a chilling rationale.

But it protects the wrong thing. Work product guards a lawyer’s own mental processes and the adversarial system. A shareholder inspection has no adversary whose preparation is being taken. What is at stake here is the quality of a board’s deliberation.

The better analogy is the deliberative-process privilege. It protects candor in collective deliberation on exactly this rationale: officials “will not communicate candidly among themselves if each remark is a potential item of discovery and front page news.”

Dep’t of Interior v. Klamath Water Users Protective Ass’n, 532 U.S. 1 (2001)

Delaware precedent is not in the way. Grimes rejected a chilling argument in 1998, but it was about a formal, counsel-prepared committee report shared with the whole board. That is a shared formal record, where the risk of opposition-chilling is near zero. It says nothing about the closed informal records that barely existed in 1998.

What restrictions could reduce the effect?

What courts do now. Standard confidentiality orders keep information from outsiders such as competitors and the general public. In Disney, for example, Chancery stopped a shareholder from publicizing what he had obtained by inspection.

Wrong audience. But for opposition-chilling the relevant audience is management. A closed record may become known to management through the production process itself: the company’s litigation team must gather and review the records, and that team answers to management.

Two possible remedies.

Identity redaction: disclose what was said, withhold who said it. It must be done by people independent of management, and it fails where the substance reveals the identity of the speaker.

Attorneys’-eyes-only: long used in discovery, but here the party to be walled off is the company’s own management. It works only with a real wall: a committee of outside directors with its own counsel.

Neither is perfect. Where the opposition-chilling cost cannot be contained, courts should apply a strong presumption against disclosure of closed records.

Between April 29 and May 15, 2026 I ran an anonymous survey of leading Delaware litigators on how Section 220 demands actually work.

They are experienced, and they come from both sides of the bar: fifteen have practiced Delaware corporate law for more than twenty years.

The sample is too small for statistical inference. Read the results as experienced practitioners describing current practice.

Demands for informal records were common before SB 21 — but production was not, and most demands never reach a judge.

Production already tracks the shared/closed line, though no decision says so. Communications with management are produced far more often than communications among outside directors, or between them and their own advisers.

Courts are well accustomed to conditioning inspection. But redacting who spoke is something almost none of them has ever seen — and walling records off from management’s own counsel, though not unknown, is far from routine.

Plaintiff-side respondents describe boards adjusting to the new statute in exactly the way the sanitization effect predicts.

Takeaways

☞︎

Two chilling effects of board transparency have gone unexamined in the literature: opposition-chilling and sanitization.

☞︎

Courts should take them into account — but they operate differently on different types of internal records.

☞︎

Informal records are less sanitized and more informative, which is a reason to facilitate access to them.

☞︎

Closed records — those not previously known to management — are highly vulnerable to opposition-chilling, which is a reason to restrict access to them.

☞︎

Delaware courts could apply most of these insights by interpreting the new “compelling need” standard.
